Dorrigo Museum

The Catalogue Handbook

How to catalogue an object with the app, how to run the collection catalogue, and how to look after the whole thing. Three parts, three different readers — start at the part that describes your job and ignore the rest.

App museum-capture.stu2038.workers.dev Worksheet eHive Cataloguing Guidelines, July 2023 Schema version 2 · 44 fields

This app replaces the two-page paper worksheet. It asks the same questions in a different order — the order you actually handle an object in — and it saves every word as you type it. Nothing about it can be broken by using it wrongly.

Which part do you need?

Part 1 if you catalogue objects. That is most people, and it is the only part you need.

Part 2 if you look things up in the catalogue, or if you are an administrator who adds people and tidies up mistakes.

Part 3 if you are the person who keeps the app running. One or two people, and they will know who they are.

Part one For volunteers

Cataloguing an object

About ten minutes an object: photograph it, answer eight short screens, read it back, save it. You do not need to be good with computers, and you are not the last line of defence — everything you save is checked by someone else.

Before you start

Three things, done once on each phone or tablet. After that you just open the app and work.

Add the app to your Home Screen

Open museum-capture.stu2038.workers.dev in Safari, tap Share, then Add to Home Screen, then Add. From then on, open the app from that icon.

This matters more than it sounds. An iPhone or iPad can clear anything saved in a Safari tab after seven days, taking your records with it. An app opened from the Home Screen is not "in Safari" and is not cleared. The app will nag you about this until you do it.

Sign in

Tap Sign in. A Cloudflare page opens, headed Log in to Dorrigo Museum Artefact Record — that is the museum's sign-in service, not a different app.

Skip the Sign in with Cloudflare button. It is meant for staff who have a Cloudflare account, and it will lead nowhere useful for a volunteer. Use the Email box underneath it: type your address, tap Send login code, and type in the code you are emailed. You are returned to the app.

That is the whole sign-in. There is no password to create and none to lose, and you will not be asked again for a long while.

You need a connection for this one step, so do it before you go into the store room. Afterwards the app works completely offline. If the app says you are not on the museum's list yet, an administrator adds you in about a minute — see Part 2.

Check the name at the top is yours

The first screen says Cataloguing as and your name. Because you signed in, the app already knows who you are — there is nothing to type.

On a museum device that other volunteers also use, the name shown is whoever used it last. Tap Someone else is cataloguing and pick yourself from the list before you start. Records are filed under whoever is named there.

Make the badge yours

Tap Change your name or colour. Pick one of the ten colours and it appears in the corner of every screen from then on — which is how you tell at a glance that the app is set to you, and not to whoever used the device last.

You can correct your own name here too. If it was spelled wrongly when you were added, this is how you fix it — and it matters, because that name goes on every record you catalogue. It's the only thing about your account you can change yourself; roles and access stay with the administrators.

What to have with you

The object. Somewhere clean and well lit to stand it. A tape measure. The register, or the object's existing tag, so you have its number. Gloves if the object needs them.

Everything starts here, and it only ever shows what this device catalogued.

The ten screens

The app asks one thing at a time. Next moves you on, Back returns, and the row of marks at the top shows how far through you are. Every word is saved the moment you type it — if the phone rings or the battery dies, the record is exactly where you left it.

The two rules that matter most

You can leave anything blank. If you don't know, move on. A blank field is honest. A guess is a problem, because the next person cannot tell it apart from a fact.

Write what you can see, not what you assume. "Stamped MADE IN ENGLAND on the base" is evidence. "English, probably Victorian" is a conclusion, and belongs only if you know it.

Which object is this?

The number from the object's tag or from the register. Type it exactly as it is written, including full stops and slashes.

The number comes first on purpose: it means your photographs can never end up filed against the wrong object. If the number is already used by another record on this device, the app says so straight away — check the register, and if it is the same object, open the existing record instead of starting a second one. The app warns you but does not stop you, because the museum's registers are the authority, not the app.

Photograph the object

See Photographs below — it is the part of the record most worth getting right.

What is it?

The everyday name. "Butter churn", not "dairy implement". Use the words someone would say out loud, because those are the words they will search for.

Describe it

What you can see: shape, colour, parts, decoration, what it is made of. Read out any writing, printing, stamps or labels exactly as they appear, odd spelling and all.

Where a question lets you list several things — materials, for instance — type one and press Enter, then the next. Each becomes its own tag, and the × removes one.

How big is it?

Height, width and length, with the unit chosen from the small box beside them. Add the weight if the object is heavy enough for it to matter.

Pick millimetres or centimetres and use the same one all day. The museum has not yet settled on a house standard — see Part 4 — so consistency within your own session is the best that can be asked for now.

What condition is it in?

Be specific. "Crack 40mm in the lid, old glue repair at the handle" is worth ten times "poor condition", because it tells the next person what to look at. Say whether the object is complete or whether pieces are missing.

Who made it, and when?

Only if you know. The date box is deliberately not a calendar: type "about 1890", "1920s", "before the war" or "unknown". Those are proper answers.

A calendar would force you to invent a precision you don't have, and invented precision in a catalogue is a lie that outlives everyone who could correct it.

What's its story?

Previous owners, how it was used, why it matters here. If you only have time for one screen, make it this one. Everything else can be worked out later from the object itself. This cannot — it is usually in one person's memory, and it leaves with them.

Where does it live?

Precise enough that someone else could find it without you: "Store room B, shelf 3, left end". Note any special handling, light or support it needs, and any exhibition it has been in.

Check the record

The whole record laid out like the paper worksheet. See Checking and saving.

The app never asks for donor details, and neither should you

No name, address, phone, email or tax number. Those are personal details about a living person, and they are kept separately by the committee with the deed of gift. Five fields on the paper form are marked this way and none of them appear in your flow. If someone tells you donor details while you are cataloguing, pass them to the committee rather than typing them anywhere in this app.

Photographs

The photographs will outlast every other part of the record. Someone in fifty years may never see this object, but they will see your picture of it.

Take four

1. The whole object. Filling the frame, plain background, nothing cropped off.

2. Maker's marks and labels. Close enough to read every word.

3. Any damage or repair. Cracks, wear, missing parts, old mends.

4. One with a ruler in shot. Settles arguments about size for good.

Getting a good one

  • Square on, from directly above. An angled photograph distorts the shape and won't match your measurements.
  • Light from the side, not from behind you. A window to one side shows shape and texture. Watch that you are not casting your own shadow across the object.
  • Never balance something fragile to get a better angle. A worse photograph is always better than a broken artefact.

Two buttons, and they do different things

Take a photo opens the camera. Choose from library picks pictures already on the device. They are separate on purpose — on an iPhone a single combined button quietly stops opening the camera at all.

Tap any thumbnail to make it the Main image — that is the one shown in the catalogue. Tap the × to remove one. Large photographs take a few seconds each and the app tells you how far through it is; you can leave the screen open while it works.

You can carry on without a photograph and add one later, but a record with no image is much harder for anyone else to check.

Checking and saving

The last screen shows the record laid out as the printed worksheet — same sections, same order, same wording. That is deliberate: you know that form, and reading a record back in its shape is how you can tell whether it is right.

Read it against the object, not against your memory of what you typed. Anything you left out shows as "not recorded", which is a perfectly good state for it to be in. Then tap Save for review.

The record is now marked for review and someone else will check it. You can reopen and change it at any time — nothing is locked, and every earlier version is kept.

If something looks wrong

My records have disappeared.

Almost always because the app was opened a different way. The Home Screen icon and a Safari tab keep separate records. Open it from the icon.

It has said "not yet backed up" for days.

Your work is safe on the device — that is where it lives first, always. But something is stopping it reaching the server. Mention it to whoever looks after the app, and keep cataloguing as normal.

There is a red notice about records not reaching the server.

The app shows that when it has been failing for six hours or more. It is telling you, not blaming you. Nothing has been lost. Report it and carry on.

I can't find someone else's record on this device.

You won't. The first screen only ever shows what was catalogued on that device. The whole collection is in the catalogue at /explore, where everyone can search it.

The registration number is already used.

Another record on this device has the same number. Check the register. If it is the same object, open the existing record instead of making a second one.

There is no signal in the store room.

Carry on. The app works completely offline and sends everything later. This is the normal case, not a fault.

I have made a mistake in a record I already saved.

Open it from the first screen and change it. Nothing is locked, and every earlier version is kept.

Someone else's name is at the top of the screen.

You are on a shared device that they used last. Tap Someone else is cataloguing and pick yourself before you start, or their name goes on your work.

It says I'm not on the museum's list.

You have proved who you are, but nobody has given you access yet. Ask an administrator to add your email address — Part 2 tells them how, and it takes a minute. The app won't start a record until then, because it would have no one to attribute it to.

I don't know the answer to a question.

Leave it blank and move on. That is the correct thing to do, not a failure to do the job.

If in doubt, stop and ask

Nothing about cataloguing is urgent. A record left half-finished while you check something costs nothing. A confident guess written into the catalogue can mislead people for decades.

Part two The catalogue & administration

The collection catalogue

At /explore. This is where the whole collection can be searched by anyone with access — and where administrators add people and tidy up mistakes. It is read-only: nobody can edit a record here, which is deliberate.

Signing in

There are no passwords in this system and none should ever be added. You type your email address, Cloudflare emails you a short code, you type the code in. That is it.

Tell people to ignore the Cloudflare button

The login page offers two ways in: a Sign in with Cloudflare button, and an email box below it. Volunteers want the email box. The button needs a Cloudflare account, which no volunteer has, so anyone who taps it reaches a dead end and concludes the app is broken.

The app and its help both say this now, but it is worth a sentence out loud when you hand someone the address: "there's a Cloudflare button — ignore it, put your email in the box underneath." The page already carries the museum's name and wording, which handles the other half of the surprise.

If you seeThen
The code never arrives Check the spelling of the address an administrator entered for you, and look in the junk folder. The email comes from Cloudflare, not from the museum.
"You don't have access yet" You proved who you are, but your email is not on the museum's list. Ask an administrator to add you — it takes about a minute.
You want to sign out Sign out at the bottom of the catalogue. It hands you back to Cloudflare, which is what actually holds the session.

Being able to receive the code is not the same as having access

Two separate gates. Cloudflare decides who may prove their identity; the museum's own user list decides who may then see anything. Both have to say yes. That is why a stranger who somehow reaches the address still gets nothing.

Finding an object

One search box: Search — name, number, maker, anything. Type a few letters and results appear as you go. There are no filters or advanced options, because they are only useful to someone who already knows what they are looking for.

Each result shows the object's main photograph, its name, its number and who catalogued it. Tap one for the full record. Tap any photograph to enlarge it, and tap again to close.

What a record page shows

  • The registration number on a tag, and the object's name.
  • Every photograph, with the main one marked.
  • Every field that was filled in, grouped as on the paper worksheet. Blank fields are simply not shown.
  • Record history: who catalogued it, its status, how many versions have been kept, and when it last changed.

Field labels come from the schema stored with the record, not from today's version. A record catalogued a year ago still reads with the wording it was written against — which is the whole reason the field definitions are kept in the database alongside the records.

Adding a photograph to an object already catalogued

Open the object and use Add photographs. Anyone except a viewer can. This is how an object gets a better picture than the one eHive holds, and the only way to photograph the objects imported from eHive at all — those records never reach a phone's capture app.

Photographs are saved on the device first and sent when there is a connection, so it works in a corner of the building with no signal. The screen says waiting until they have gone; you can close the app and they will still be there.

Nothing is replaced

A new photograph is added alongside the existing ones, never over them. Use Make main to choose which picture represents the object in the collection — that is a cataloguing decision, so any volunteer can make it, and it can be changed again at any time.

Roles

Every person on the list has one of three roles. The wording below is what the app itself shows when you add someone.

RoleCan do
Viewer Search and read records.
Volunteer All of the above, see who catalogued each object, add photographs to a record and choose its main image.
Admin All of the above, and add and remove people, remove records from the collection, and put removed records back.

Give out Admin sparingly — two or three people, so that no single person leaving locks the museum out. The very first person to sign in became an administrator automatically, and only while the list was empty; after that, every administrator is made by another administrator.

Adding and removing people

Manage who has access at the bottom of the catalogue — administrators only.

Enter their email address and name

The address must be the one they will actually receive the code at. A misspelling here is the single commonest reason someone says "no code arrived".

Get the name as close as you can, but don't agonise: they can correct their own name in the app once they sign in, and choose their own badge colour. That is the only part of their account they can change — roles and access remain yours.

Choose what they can do

Viewer, volunteer or admin, as above. It can be changed later by adding them again with a different role.

Tap Add this person

They can sign in immediately. There is nothing to send them and no password to issue — tell them the web address and that they will be emailed a code.

This one step grants both things at once: they can search the catalogue, and they can catalogue objects in the app. Their name also appears in the "who is cataloguing" list on shared museum devices. Somebody who has not been added can do neither, which is the point.

The list below shows everyone with access, their role, and when they were last here — or "not signed in yet", which is useful when someone says they cannot get in. Remove access takes someone off the list. You cannot remove yourself, and the app will not let the last administrator be removed.

Removing a record, and putting it back

Removing is not deaccessioning

This is for duplicates, test entries and mistakes. If the museum is actually disposing of an object, that is a committee decision and it belongs in the museum's own deaccession process — not in a button in an app.

Open the record and tap Remove this record from the collection. There are two deliberate speed bumps:

  • A reason. "Duplicate of 1994.016" or "test record made during training". Six months later, "why is 1994.017 missing?" needs a better answer than someone's memory.
  • Typing the registration number to confirm. Removing the wrong record should be harder than a mis-tapped button.

Nothing is destroyed. The record, its history and its photographs all remain — it simply stops appearing in the collection, and it also disappears from the device that catalogued it, so nobody is left looking at a record the museum has retired.

Removed records are listed at the bottom of Manage who has access, each with its reason, who removed it, when, and how many photographs were kept. Put it back restores one completely.

Part three For the caretaker

Looking after the app

For whoever keeps this running. You do not need to be a developer to do most of it, but you will need the project on a computer and a Cloudflare account with access to the museum's resources.

Where it lives

PieceWhere
The app and its APIA Cloudflare Worker named museum-capture
RecordsCloudflare D1 database artefact-catalogue
Photographs and backupsCloudflare R2 bucket artefact-photos
Sign-inCloudflare Access, email one-time code
The questions themselvesschema/worksheet.v2.yaml in the project, and a copy in the database

One assumption holds the whole thing up

Cloudflare Access sits in front of the API and identifies the caller. The Worker trusts the email address in the header because Access put it there. If the Worker is ever exposed without Access in front of it, anyone can set that header themselves and the door is open. Never move or narrow the Access application without understanding that.

How records travel

A record is written to the device first and always. Sync is a background convenience, never a precondition for cataloguing — a volunteer with no signal must be able to work all morning, and does.

  • The app tries to sync when it opens, whenever the connection comes back, and every five minutes.
  • Records go up in batches of twenty. Photographs follow, once each.
  • A device pulls back only what it catalogued, not the whole collection — that is what stops every phone slowly filling up with the entire museum.
  • Donor fields are stripped before anything is sent. They never reach the server at all.
  • Nothing overwrites anything: every save appends a new version, and every earlier version is kept.

Two names on every record, and only one of them is proof

A record carries who catalogued it and, separately, which signed-in account sent it. They are not the same claim and the difference is deliberate:

Stored asWhat it means
captured_by The email of the person who did the work, chosen from the registered list. On a personal phone that is simply the person signed in. On a shared device it is whoever picked their name — a claim, made in good faith, but a claim.
synced_by The account whose sign-in carried the record to the server. The Worker writes this from the Cloudflare Access header; a device cannot assert it or change it. This is the one that is proof.

When they agree, attribution is verified and the catalogue shows one name. When they differ — the museum iPad, signed in as itself, used by a named volunteer — the record shows both rather than pretending to a certainty nobody has. Records made before this change keep a typed name and no verified account, which is correct: they never had one, and back-filling a guess would erase the distinction.

Access must cover the whole site

In Cloudflare Zero Trust, the Access application must match the whole hostname, not just /explore and /api. If the capture app at / is left outside the policy, anyone with the address can open it — and the app can no longer tell who they are, so it will refuse to start a record. Check this after any change to the Access configuration.

Badge colours

Each person's badge colour is stored as a name — "sage", "ochre" — not as a colour value, so the palette can be restyled without rewriting anybody's row. Somebody who has never chosen one still gets a consistent colour derived from their email address, so a shared device shows several different circles rather than a row of identical grey ones. The ten colours live in src/avatars.ts.

Sending records to eHive

Send records to eHive at the bottom of the catalogue, admins only. It builds the data for eHive's own import workbook and tells you what to do with it.

It makes a file. It doesn't send anything.

eHive has no way for another system to write into it. Imports are run by Vernon Systems staff, against a test server first, from a spreadsheet you email them with the photographs alongside. The last step of this process is a person, and no amount of software changes that.

The warning worth reading

Several fields — object type, place made, maker, location — are pick lists in eHive. A value that doesn't exactly match a term already in the museum's account creates a new one. So "Jugs" and "jugs" become two terms, and nobody notices until the vocabulary is a mess.

The screen lists every value with either in eHive or new term, checked against the terms the museum actually uses, and shows the existing spelling where it can find a close match. Values marked new term are sometimes genuinely new and sometimes a second spelling of something you already have — only a person can tell which, which is why the app flags them rather than silently correcting them.

Records that came from eHive

The museum's existing eHive records are in the collection, imported from an XML report. Each keeps eHive's own record id, so if one is corrected here a later export updates the original rather than creating a second copy. They are marked exported, which is simply true.

Some eHive fields have no counterpart in this app — its measurements are one line of text where we hold height, width and length separately. Nothing was discarded: the original eHive record is kept verbatim and can still be read.

Get a copy off Cloudflare

Download a snapshot to a committee member's machine monthly, and keep something physical in the museum safe. Two copies on one platform under one account is one lapsed billing away from zero copies. This is the single most important item in Part 3.

Checking it is healthy

Run these from the project folder. They read; they change nothing.

# The last 20 records to arrive — the quickest "is it working?"
npm run db:records

# Any sync failures, newest first, with the device that hit them
npm run db:errors

# Confirms the field definitions are loaded in the database
npm run db:check

To see who has actually been cataloguing, and how recently, the database carries a cataloguer_activity view — one row per person, with their record count and the date of their last one. It is the quickest answer to "is anyone using this?".

If a volunteer reports records not backing up, npm run db:errors is where to look first, and npm run db:records tells you whether anybody's records are arriving or nobody's.

Releasing a change

npm install
npm run typecheck     # app and server; both must pass
npm run deploy        # builds, then publishes

Volunteers' phones pick up a new version on their next reload — the app is built so a fix actually reaches a device that has already been used, which is not automatic for an offline-capable app and was got wrong once already.

If you change the questions, edit schema/worksheet.v2.yaml and then run npm run db:seed so the database has the matching definitions. Changing questions anywhere else in the code is the wrong move — the whole app is driven from that one file.

Known issues

Someone is signed in but the app won't let them start a record.

Their email is not on the museum's user list. Add them in Manage who has access and they can start straight away. Cloudflare letting them prove who they are is a separate gate from the museum letting them in.

A record opened on a second device does not show photographs taken on the first.

Photograph details are not yet pulled back down. The photographs themselves are safe on the server and appear correctly in the catalogue at /explore.

Two records have no photograph.

The two blazers, M1720 and M1723. Their images exist but are held back until the museum confirms their accession numbers against the register — a photograph filed under a wrong number is harder to find and fix than one never filed.

A photograph can't be added to a record after it leaves the device that made it.

Records imported from eHive never reach a phone, and the catalogue is read-only. The 35 imported records got their images by a one-off script. The general case isn't built yet.

There is no voice recording.

Also deliberate. The groundwork is in place — each screen already carries the single open question a volunteer would answer aloud — but nothing records or transcribes yet.

Part four Decisions & glossary

Still to be decided

Six questions the museum has not answered yet. They are recorded in the schema rather than guessed at, because cataloguing conventions are institution-specific and expensive to unpick once a few hundred records exist. Until they are settled, this manual cannot be more specific than it is.

QuestionWhy it matters
Millimetres or centimetres? Volunteers are currently told to pick one and stay consistent. A house standard would let the app enforce it and make measurements comparable across the collection.
Is there a pattern for Dorrigo numbers? The museum's 35 eHive records are all M followed by a number, sometimes with a letter suffix — M12, M654a. If that is the house rule the app could check it and catch typos as they are made. Numbers are accepted as free text until somebody confirms it.
Who assigns the registration number? The app assumes a number already exists on the object's tag or in the register. If a committee member assigns them beforehand, that is fine; if the app should issue them, that is a different design.
What is the ruled line after "Unknown" on the acquisition tickbox? Elaboration on "unknown", or an "Other" option? It changes what that field is.
Do cataloguing volunteers ever touch the donor block? Currently, firmly no. Worth confirming that matches how the museum actually works.
Storage and display eHive keeps these as two separate fields; the worksheet asks one question, which currently feeds only storage. Split the question, or accept that display requirements sit under storage?
Pick list vocabularies Should the museum write term lists for object type, place made and location for the app to offer, or keep free text and tidy the duplicates in eHive after each import?
Two errors in eHive itself M1723 is used by two different objects, and one record has no object number at all. Neither is an app problem, but both are cheap to fix at 35 records and expensive at 500.
